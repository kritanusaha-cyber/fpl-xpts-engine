I. Why the model simulates

FPL pays on thresholds, not rates. A clean sheet is all or nothing. DefCon pays at ten actions and nothing at nine. Goals conceded cost a point every second goal.

The mean of a threshold is not the threshold of the mean. So a model that averages first and applies the rules second gets the wrong answer, and the error depends on the player. It overrates the steady and underrates the explosive.

This model therefore simulates. It draws 20,000 versions of each fixture and scores every draw with the real rules. That produces a distribution, not a number. The distribution is what captaincy and risk decisions need.

Read this first. 2026/27 has not started. Every projection runs on priors. Nothing here is validated against this season. All out-of-sample results come from 2016/17–2025/26.

II. Defensive contribution

Nobody publishes the DefCon rule. The model recovers it from the data, and it is position-dependent:

PositionActions that countThreshold
DEFtackles + CBI10
MID / FWDtackles + CBI + recoveries12

Defenders get nothing for recoveries. That single fact decides the archetype. A centre-back earns DefCon by contesting the ball — blocking, clearing, tackling. He earns nothing for collecting it. Counting recoveries inflates defender totals by about half and turns every ball-playing defender into a false target. The rule reconstructs exactly on all 26,330 rows of 2025/26.

This changes where you shop. DefCon defenders play for sides that defend deep. A good side keeps the ball, so its defenders never reach the threshold. Brighton's centre-backs average 6.9 defensive actions per 90 against a league mean of 7.7. Strong team, poor DefCon source.

III. Attacking returns

A. Shares, not rates

A striker's goals per 90 already contains his team. Multiply that rate by a fixture-adjusted team strength and you count the team twice. A Manchester City forward gets credit for City's chance creation once in his own rate and again in the multiplier.

The model uses shares instead:

E[player xG] = projected team xG × shrunk share of team xG

A share says "this player takes 24% of his side's chances". That travels. It survives a fixture change. It survives a transfer.

The share structure lifts correlation with real output from 0.375 to 0.462. The gain carries through to actual goals in every outfield position: midfielders +13.5%, forwards +13.6%, defenders +8.4%.

B. Shrinkage

A player with 200 minutes has not proved anything. The model pulls his share toward his position and price group:

w = n90 / (n90 + σ²w / σ²b)

The model estimates the variance ratio per position rather than assuming it. Midfielders come out at 6.5, defenders and forwards at 15.7. Midfielders separate from each other more than twice as fast. A midfielder proves himself in half a season; a forward needs a full one.

C. Penalties

FPL's expected-goals figure includes penalties. That inflates every designated taker. Worse, it follows the wrong player when duty changes hands in the summer.

Igor Thiago projected the second-highest xG share in the league. Nine penalties drove it.

The model strips penalty xG out at 0.79 per attempt and adds it back as a separate term. The term attaches to whoever takes them now, read from the API's penalty order. Thiago's share falls 23%, Palmer's 30%, Fernandes's 27%.

D. Set pieces

Set pieces generate 26.8% of all league xG. The model previously treated them as open play. They are not the same thing, and the difference is a football one.

A hard fixture suppresses open play far more than it suppresses corners. Hold a side to one goal and it still wins set pieces. So the model now allocates open-play goals out of the drawn scoreline and draws set-piece goals from the player's own rate.

Set-piece involvement is a stable trait, unlike finishing:

IV. Minutes

A. Two decisions, two models

A manager makes two separate calls. He picks a starting eleven on squad status and fixture. He takes players off on scoreline and legs.

The model uses two chained logits, P(appear) then P(≥60 | appear), rather than one ordered model. Forcing both calls through one linear index costs accuracy on both.

Beating the base rate proves nothing. A decayed average already reaches 43%. The real number is 21.6% better than the best single-feature baseline.

B. Only one goalkeeper plays

The model estimates each player independently. Nothing stopped it projecting three starting keepers at Arsenal, and it did exactly that.

Minutes now compete. Within each club and position the model shares out the slots a real eleven fills — one keeper, four defenders, four midfielders, two forwards — weighting each player by p³.

The exponent is football, not statistics. Managers do not spread minutes evenly. Simple proportional scaling handed a third of the goalkeeping to backups and dropped Raya to 0.57. Sharpening concentrates minutes on the first choice (0.90) and strips the reserves (0.04). That is how a squad actually works.

V. Team goals

The model uses Dixon–Coles: two Poissons plus a correction on the four lowest scorelines.

λ = exp(attack_h − defence_a + home), μ = exp(attack_a − defence_h)
τ(0,0) = 1 − λμρ, τ(1,1) = 1 − ρ

Football is not independent. A side that goes ahead shuts the game down. Two sides level late both stop taking risks. So 0–0 and 1–1 happen more often than independent scoring rates predict. Skip the correction and you underprice every clean sheet.

The model decays old matches at exp(−0.003 × days). It tuned that rate out of sample and found a clean minimum. The half-life works out near eight months, which is about how long a new manager or a tactical change takes to show in results.

Fitting on xG beats fitting on goals (log-loss 0.9978 against 1.0093). xG uses every chance. Goals use only the ones that went in.

The model adds nothing to the betting market. Blending it with de-vigged closing odds gains +0.00101 log-loss, t = +0.58, CI [−0.0024, +0.0044]. Nothing, on 758 fixtures. The market already knows what the model knows about team strength. Dixon–Coles survives for one reason only: bookmakers do not price fixtures six gameweeks out, and the planner needs them.

VI. Defensive counts and bonus

A. Why negative binomial

A Poisson assumes variance equals mean. That would hold if every player faced the same defensive workload each week. They do not. A holding midfielder against a possession side doubles his own average. The same player in a comfortable home win records almost nothing.

SpecificationBrierBias
negative binomial, projected minutes0.0993−0.002
Poisson, projected minutes0.1010−0.028
negative binomial, flat 90 minutes0.1270+0.088
base rate0.11830.000

Poisson is not just worse. It is biased downward, because it cannot produce the long right tail that a threshold pays on.

Evaluating at a flat 90 minutes scores worse than guessing the base rate. You reach a threshold by playing. A substitute never gets there. This is why the minutes model comes first.

DefCon is the weakest component. Above p = 0.5 the calibration breaks. In the 0.6–0.8 band the real hit rate was 0.000. The model now recalibrates and caps the tail at 0.40. DefCon supplies about 10% of a typical starter's projection and 9% of the largest mispricings, so the headline picks do not rest on it.

B. Bonus

BPS is a fixed function of the match action log, so bonus should compute exactly. Ranking by true BPS recovers 100% of bonus winners across 760 fixtures. The award logic is right.

The inputs are the problem. FPL stopped publishing key passes, dribbles, crosses, fouls and big chances after 2018/19.

Each player panel carries a six-by-five grid over the attacking half. Three colours mark whether he shoots from a zone more than his position does, about the same, or less. Three categories read faster across thirty cells than a shading ramp does.

The half-spaces run between the centre and the touchline. A player receiving there can face goal, shoot, or cut the ball back. The touchline never traps him. Modern sides overload these channels deliberately.

Zone 14 sits centrally, just outside the box. It produces more shots and assists per touch than anywhere else in open play. A midfielder who lives in Zone 14 creates chances by definition.

The reads come out right. Saka takes 70% of his shots from the half-spaces and 27% centrally — a winger cutting in from the right. Van Dijk is 93% central and 90% inside the box — a centre-back attacking crosses. Haaland is 90% box and 7% Zone 14 — a pure penalty-box striker.

These are shot locations, not touches. A true touch heatmap needs coordinate-level event data. A partial collection exists (154 players, 7,382 coordinates) but no full season.

C. Arriving is not supplying

Box touches predict next-half chances created with extra R² = 0.258 beyond final-third passes (t = 8.67). The two measure different jobs. A midfielder who passes into the final third supplies. A midfielder who gets into the box arrives. The arriving midfielder scores and creates more than his passing suggests.

Saka sits in the 98th percentile for box touches and the 16th for final-third passes. He gets there. He does not build.

D. Full-backs assist from wide

The obvious theory says an advanced full-back assists because he pushes high. The data says the mechanism is crossing:

A full-back assists from wide, not from the area. Arriving in the box is a winger's pattern. For a defender it is mostly noise. So crossing drives the creativity term for defenders, and box touches drive it for everyone else.

VIII. Goalkeeping

PsxG and xGOT are the same thing: a shot's value recomputed after it leaves the boot, using where it crossed the line. For a keeper this is the right denominator. It asks the only fair question — given the shots he actually faced, how many would an average keeper concede?

goals prevented = PsxG faced − goals conceded

Save percentage cannot answer that. A keeper behind a poor defence faces better chances. He posts a worse save rate while playing better. PsxG removes the defence from the measurement.

The model attributes each shot to the keeper who faced it, not to the defending team. Lammens prevented 7.05 goals, Verbruggen 5.59, Donnarumma 5.04. Vicario conceded 5.07 more than expected.

A. Two ways to judge a keeper, and the gap between them

There are two honest measures of shot-stopping, and they disagree in a useful way.

GSAA = saves made − (shots faced × league save rate)
goals prevented = PsxG faced − goals conceded

GSAA compares a keeper to the league. It asks how many saves an average keeper makes from that many shots, and counts the difference. The league save rate on shots that reach a keeper is 69%. GSAA applies that flat rate to everyone.

Goals prevented compares him to his own shots. It asks what an average keeper concedes from the specific chances he faced.

The gap between the two is the defence. A keeper behind a poor back line faces better chances, so a flat league rate is too easy a bar and GSAA understates him. A keeper behind a good one faces weak shots, and GSAA flatters him.

Lammens and Pope are the case for carrying both. On GSAA, Pope looks like the better keeper by +1.7. Adjust for what each actually faced and Lammens is ahead by 8.7 goals. Lammens dealt with the hardest shots in the league; Pope was handed the easiest and still conceded more than expected.

Both numbers appear on the keeper panel, with the gap named. Neither is the "right" one. GSAA is what a keeper did; goals prevented is what he did given his circumstances.

B. Shot quality faced separates two defensive jobs

Goals conceded tells you a defence let goals in. It does not tell you why. Two defences with the same total do different jobs. One concedes many weak shots. The other concedes few strong ones.

PsxG/SOT = PsxG faced ÷ shots that reached the keeper

This splits what a keeper faces into volume and quality. A low PsxG/SOT means the defence forces bad shots. Attackers reach dangerous areas rarely, or reach them under pressure and scuff it.

The league average is 0.317. Roughly a third of the shots that reach a keeper are expected to go in, which matches a league save rate near 70%.

C. Two counting mistakes that changed the answer

The first version of this analysis was wrong twice, and both errors are easy to repeat.

A shot's team id names the side that took it, not the side that faced it. Grouping on it measures shots created and calls them shots conceded. The defending side is the other team in the fixture.

Half the shots flagged on target never reach the keeper. A defender blocks them. The flag records where the ball was heading, not where it arrived, and those shots carry PsxG of zero. Leaving them in the denominator dragged the league average down to 0.160 — half its true value.

Both mistakes produced numbers that looked plausible. The check that caught them was comparing the team figure against the keeper figure for the same season. They disagreed, and only one could be right.

D. Defences control volume more than quality

Volume persists across half a season. Quality does not.

A good defence prevents shots. It does not make shots much worse. Once the ball is struck and reaches the keeper, its danger is mostly the shooter's doing and the position he shot from. Defending happens earlier, in stopping the chance from existing at all.

Twenty teams is a thin test. Volume clears significance and quality does not, which is the finding. But nothing here predicts second-half clean sheet rate at this sample size, including volume. The model therefore prices clean sheets from team strength and the Dixon–Coles scoreline, and shows PsxG/SOT as context for reading a keeper — is he overworked, or left exposed — rather than as a forecast input.
The model does not use shot-stopping. It does not persist across half a season — and neither does the control, which is the difficulty a keeper faces. When the control fails, the test has no power. The honest statement is that one season cannot measure shot-stopping, not that the skill is absent. PsxG also loses to plain goals conceded when predicting future goals conceded (r = +0.503 against +0.657), because it counts only shots on target and throws away blocked and wayward ones. PsxG belongs to the keeper. It does not measure a defence.

IX. Pricing

The first version divided surplus by the budget shadow price. That gave a £4.5m defender a £13.3m fair price.

The error was conceptual. The shadow price is a marginal rate at a constrained optimum, not the price of output. It came out at 1.11 xPts per £1m over six gameweeks. The market's real gradient for defenders is about 3.2. Dividing by a number three times too small tripled every gap.

A linear map is also the wrong shape. FPL prices with diminishing returns — premium players are charged for a ceiling that is itself capped. So the model fits a curve per position and inverts it:

points per gameweek = a + b log(price) → fair price = exp((points − a) / b)

Fair price now answers a market question: at what price has FPL historically delivered this rate, for this position? Luke Shaw moves from an absurd £13.3m to £6.6m against a £4.5m listing. That is a claim you can act on.

A. Roles, not positions

surplus = xPts − λB price − μP − replacement in role

μP prices the squad quota. FPL forces 2/5/5/3, so you never pick a forward against the whole market. You pick him against the other forwards you must field. Drop this term and 76% of goalkeepers look good against 1% of forwards. That describes the constraint, not the players.

The model also compares within role, not FPL position. "MID" holds Saka and Zubimendi. It clusters players on goal threat, creation and defensive workload, which recovers the real groups: MID · high goal threat is the wingers, DEF · high goal threat is the set-piece centre-backs.

XI. Leakage control

Every feature function passes through a decorator. The decorator filters its input to earlier gameweeks and then audits what comes back. Checking inputs alone is not enough — a function can rejoin the full table and drag the future in with it.

All testing walks forward. Random k-fold on football panel data leaks the future and inflates every number.

XII. Picking the squad: rank, not points

The squad shown above is not the one with the most expected points. It is the one chosen by a different objective, and the difference is worth about a point and a half of projection.

You are not scored on points. You are scored on where you finish, which means what matters is your margin over everyone else. Write that margin across every player in the game and it comes to the sum of (do you own him − how much of the field owns him) × his points. The second half of that does not depend on your choices at all, so on average, chasing rank and chasing points are the same thing.

They stop being the same thing because finishing high does not need a positive margin, it needs a big one. That is about the spread of your margin, not its average. And the spread has a property worth knowing: because you either own a player or you do not, the maths stays simple, and each player changes your spread by an amount that depends on how widely he is owned.

The sign is what matters. A player more than half the field owns reduces your spread, because when he blanks everyone blanks with you and nothing moves. A player nobody owns carries the full swing on his own.

Tested over four seasons, the best setting is to welcome that swing in players the field holds, and avoid it in players held alone. That beat the field in four seasons from four, worth about 72 points a season. Chasing points alone won two. The common advice that differentials win rank is backwards here: a blank from a player nobody owns costs you places, a blank from a player everybody owns costs you nothing.

This is why the squad sometimes prefers a well-owned, streaky forward to a quieter differential projected slightly higher. See Findings for the sweep and the two controls that were needed before it was believed.

XIII. Players who changed club

A player's record is a record of a player in a situation. Move him and part of it stops applying — and the part that stops applying is not the same for every kind of scoring.

Take a midfielder who was cheap and reliable at a team that defends deep. He hit the defensive threshold most weeks because his team spent most of the match without the ball. Sign him for a side that dominates possession and he will not, however good he is. That is his team's behaviour, not his.

So the model measures it. Across six seasons, a club's midfielders make defensive actions at a rate that tracks how much of the ball the club has — from 0.78 times the league rate at the most possession-heavy sides to 1.10 at the deepest-sitting ones. A move between those two ends multiplies a player's defensive rate by about 0.77, which can more than halve his chance of hitting the threshold. The correction is the ratio between his new club and his old one.

Goals and assists work differently. His old share of chances tells you what he did when the alternatives were his old team-mates; it does not know he now plays behind a better forward line. So the model also looks at the player whose place he is taking — the same job at the same club — and blends the two halfway.

Tested on 55 players who actually changed club, that blend predicts their new output 21% better than their own record alone. Using only the incumbent is worse too. Both halves carry real information, which is why neither is used on its own.

One thing this deliberately does not do: it is not applied to players with no Premier League history. That was tried and it failed — a new signing's price already tells you what the club thinks it is buying, and it tells you more than the profile of the man he replaces.

XIV. Keeping up with the data

The numbers here move for three reasons: a gameweek finishes, bonus points land, or prices and injury news change overnight. All three matter, and all three are worth rebuilding for.

A watcher checks the game's feed every twenty minutes and compares it against what it saw last time. Almost always nothing has moved and it does nothing, which is the point — the check costs two small requests, while a rebuild re-simulates all 38 gameweeks. When something has moved, the whole chain runs and the page republishes on its own.

Ownership is deliberately left out of that trigger. It drifts every few minutes and would fire the rebuild constantly, and only the squad-selection objective reads it.

This cannot run inside your browser. The game's API refuses cross-origin requests, so a page cannot fetch its own live data — the work has to happen before the page is served rather than inside it. The consequence worth knowing: the page is only as fresh as the last rebuild. So it says so. The line under the title gives the age of the build and turns red past a day and a half, because a stale projection looks exactly like a current one otherwise.

XVII. What failed

A. xGOT as a skill measure

xGOT should measure finishing. It does not persist.

Signalr, first half → secondt
npxG per shot (control)+0.7429.05
finishing per shot−0.065−0.54

The control matters. Shot quality persists strongly, so the test can detect persistence when it is there. Finishing and placement do not persist, and neither adds anything once you know xG.

A player's shot selection is a skill. What he does to the ball afterwards is close to noise.

B. Rank tilting

Write your margin over the field as a sum over every player:

margin = ∑i (owni − EOi) × pointsi

The field term does not depend on your choices. So maximising rank and maximising points are the same problem in expectation. A differential can only help through the non-linearity: a top finish needs a large margin, and large margins need variance.

Tilting toward lightly-owned players costs points at every dose above 0.05, and the damage grows: −524 points at k = 0.30, −856 at k = 1.00. Tilting does buy variance (weekly SD 18.4 to 20.6) but it costs far more in mean (+0.09 to −6.9). The upper tail never improves.

Ownership tracks quality. Heavily-owned players are owned because they are good. Fading them means holding worse players.

C. Taking hits

The obvious rule says take a −4 when the weekly gain times the holding period beats 4 points. That rule cost 349 points across four seasons against simply declining every hit. Only 156 of that was the fees.

Demanding a bigger margin recovers the loss monotonically, and only reaches zero when you stop taking hits entirely. That pattern is the optimiser's curse. You pick the transfer with the largest estimated gain, and the largest estimate in a noisy set is too high. You pay four points to buy estimation error. Raising the bar does not fix it, because the bar filters on the same bad estimate.

XVIII. Known limits

  • Forwards project 0.30 points low per gameweek and midfielders 0.18. Defenders are unbiased. The model overall runs 0.12 low, about 10% of the mean.
  • Gameweeks are simulated independently and added. That is right for the mean and understates variance, because it ignores form and rotation running in streaks.
  • DefCon has one season of data. Its relationship with clean sheets measures near zero, but the covariate that should drive it — opponent possession — is not in the FPL feed.
  • League-strength numbers for foreign signings are assumptions, not fitted values.
  • The price curve explains only 23–35% of variance. Price predicts points weakly, which is exactly why mispricing exists — but it makes fair price a central tendency, not a target.
  • Separating a real edge of the size measured here would take roughly ten seasons. Any edge claimed from one season is noise.
