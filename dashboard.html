Shadow Price

Fair price inverts the LP reduced cost into pounds: price + (xPts − λ·price − μpos − replacement) / λ. λ prices the budget and μ the positional quota, because FPL forces a 2/5/5/3 squad. Replacement is measured within role, not within FPL position — "MID" holds both wingers and holding midfielders, and ranking them together compares people doing different jobs. Roles are clustered from goal threat, creation and defensive workload.

Risk against reward

Rightward is more expected points over the horizon; upward is more volatile. Useful picks sit low and right. Captaincy wants the opposite corner.

The premise

Most FPL scoring rules are thresholds, not rates. A clean sheet is binary. DefCon pays at 10 actions and nothing at 9. Goals conceded dock a point every second goal. The mean of a threshold is not the threshold of the mean, so a model that projects averages and then applies the rules will be wrong in a direction that depends on the player — it overrates the consistent and underrates the volatile.

So this simulates. 20,000 fixtures per match, scored with the real rules on each draw. The statistical work below is almost entirely about getting the inputs right; the arithmetic afterwards is trivial and never in doubt.

Read this first. 2026/27 has no completed gameweeks. Every projection runs on priors alone and none of it has been validated against this season. The out-of-sample results below come from 2016/17–2025/26.

Getting the inputs right

DefCon: which actions count, and for whom

Nobody publishes the definition, so it was recovered from the data — and it is position-dependent in a way that matters tactically:

PositionQualifying actionsThreshold
DEFtackles + clearances, blocks, interceptions10
MID / FWDtackles + CBI + recoveries12

Defenders do not get credit for recoveries. That is the whole ballgame for the archetype: a centre-back is rewarded for contesting the ball (blocks, clearances, tackles), not for picking up loose ones. Including recoveries — the intuitive thing to do — inflates defender counts by roughly half and would have made every ball-playing defender look like a DefCon target. Verified exact on all 26,330 rows of 2025/26.

The consequence for team selection: DefCon defenders are found at sides that defend deep and concede territory, not at sides that dominate possession. Brighton's centre-backs average 6.9 defensive actions per 90 against a league mean of 7.7 — a good team, a poor DefCon source, because they spend the match with the ball.

Attacking returns: shares, not rates

The single most important input decision here. A player's goals per 90 already contains the quality of the team he played for. Multiply that by a fixture-adjusted team strength and you have counted the team twice — a Manchester City forward gets credited for City's chance creation once in his own rate and again in the fixture multiplier.

E[player xG] = projected_team_xG x shrunk_player_share_of_team_xG

A share is approximately invariant to team strength: it says "this player takes 24% of his side's chances", which travels when the fixture changes and when he transfers. Measured, the share structure lifts correlation with realised output 0.3752 → 0.4622 (+23%), and the gain survives all the way through to realised goals in every outfield position (MID +13.5%, FWD +13.6%, DEF +8.4%).

Penalties are a different event and must be modelled separately

FPL's expected_goals includes penalties. A designated taker therefore looks like a better open-play threat than he is, and — worse — the inflation follows the wrong player when duty changes hands over the summer. Igor Thiago was projecting the second-highest xG share in the league, largely on nine penalties.

Penalty xG is stripped out (0.79 per attempt, from FBref) and added back as its own term, attached to whoever holds the duty now via the API's penalties_order. Effect on shares: Thiago −23%, Palmer −30%, Fernandes −27%.

Minutes: selection and substitution are different decisions

Modelled as two chained logits, P(appear) then P(≥60 | appear), rather than one ordered model. A manager decides who starts on squad status and fixture; he decides who comes off on scoreline and legs. Forcing both through one linear index costs calibration on both.

Only one goalkeeper plays

Per-player priors are estimated independently, which means nothing stops a model projecting three starting keepers at one club — and it did. Start probabilities are now allocated within each club and position across the slots a real XI fills (1 GKP, 4 DEF, 4 MID, 2 FWD), weighted by p³.

The exponent is football, not statistics: minutes do not distribute smoothly across a squad. Proportional scaling handed a third of the goalkeeping minutes to backups and dropped Raya to 0.57; sharpening concentrates them on the established starter (0.90) and strips the reserves (0.04), which is how a manager actually picks a team.

Low scores are correlated, so the Poisson needs correcting

Team goals use Dixon-Coles, which is two Poissons plus a correction on the four lowest scorelines:

tau(0,0) = 1 - lambda*mu*rho tau(1,1) = 1 - rho

0–0 and 1–1 occur more often than independent scoring rates imply, because football is not independent: a side that goes ahead closes the game down, and two sides at 0–0 late both stop taking risks. Without the correction, clean sheets are systematically underpriced.

Time decay of exp(-0.003 * days) was tuned out of sample, not assumed — a clean minimum, giving squads a memory of roughly eight months, which is about how long a managerial or tactical change takes to show up in results. Fitting on xG rather than goals wins (log-loss 0.9978 vs 1.0093): xG converges faster because it uses every chance rather than only the ones that went in.

Negative result, kept. Blending this model with de-vigged bookmaker odds adds +0.00101 log-loss, t = +0.58, CI [−0.0024, +0.0044] — nothing, on 758 fixtures. The market already knows what the model knows about team strength. Dixon-Coles earns its place only because bookmakers post no lines six gameweeks out, which is the horizon decisions are made on.

Defensive actions are overdispersed because roles differ

DefCon counts are modelled negative binomial, not Poisson. A Poisson assumes variance equals mean, which would be true if every player faced the same defensive workload every week. They do not: a holding midfielder against a possession side racks up double his own average, and the same player in a comfortable home win records almost nothing.

Small samples (37 rows), but that band is exactly where a DefCon strategy shops. DefCon is ~10% of a typical starter's projection and ~9% of the largest mispricings, so the headline outliers do not rest on it.

Bonus is deterministic — but the inputs are gone

BPS is a fixed function of the action log, so bonus should be exactly computable. Ranking by true BPS recovers 100% of bonus winners (760/760 fixtures), which proves the award and tie logic. The problem is upstream: FPL stopped publishing key passes, dribbles, crosses, fouls and big chances after 2018/19.

Pricing: what the market charges for output

Fair price was originally price + surplus / lambda, using the budget shadow price from the optimiser. That was wrong, and it is the error that produced a £13.3m fair price for a £4.5m defender.

The football reason: lambda is the marginal rate at a constrained optimum, not the price of output. It came out at 1.11 xPts per £1m over six gameweeks, while the market's realised gradient for defenders is about 3.2. Dividing by a number three times too small tripled every price gap.

Worse, a linear map assumes constant returns, and FPL is explicitly priced with diminishing ones — premium players are being charged for attacking ceiling, which is itself capped. So the curve is fitted per position and inverted:

points_per_gameweek = a + b * log(price) -> fair price = exp((points - a) / b)
Position£4.5m buys£9.0m buysmarginal
DEF1.864.73steepest — cheap defenders are genuinely poor
MID1.674.16steep
FWD1.753.82flattest — cheap forwards still score

Fair price now answers a market question: at what price has FPL historically delivered this rate, for this position? Luke Shaw moves from an absurd £13.3m to £6.6m against a £4.5m listing — a real, actionable claim about a nailed cheap defender rather than an artefact of the wrong denominator.

Positional quotas, and why roles are not positions

The separate "value vs role" figure is a linear-programming reduced cost:

surplus = xPts_H - lambda*price - mu_position - replacement_in_role

mu is the dual on the squad quota. FPL forces 2/5/5/3, so you are never choosing a forward against the whole market, only against the other forwards you are compelled to field. Omitting it made 76% of goalkeepers look positive against 1% of forwards — a statement about the constraint, not the players.

Roles are clustered from play style, because FPL's four positions are a scoring construct rather than a football one. "MID" contains both Saka and Zubimendi. Clustering on goal threat, creation and defensive workload recovers recognisable groups: MID · high goal threat is the wingers; DEF · high goal threat is the set-piece centre-backs. Replacement level is measured inside those groups.

These labels were wrong once. An earlier version guessed football names from cluster centroids and produced "attacking full-back" for two centre-backs, and put Haaland in "support forward". The clusters were real; the names were invented. They are now derived mechanically from the dominant axis.

New signings

187 of 592 players have never played in the league. Two priors were tested on 244 genuine debutants:

position × price tier0.0297baseline

Role inheritance failed, and the reason is football: FPL's price already encodes the club's own judgement of what the signing is for. A £5.0m centre-back is priced as a starter because the club bought him to start. The role profile adds noise on top — and because it is minutes-weighted it inherits the first-choice player's share, which handed a backup striker Watkins's 0.324.

Foreign output worked, after heavy discounting. Big-5 non-penalty output predicts Premier League share at corr +0.656, but the fitted slope is 0.40 — meaning scoring abroad overstates Premier League share by about 2.5x even after a league-strength adjustment. Players do not carry their rate across; they carry their rank.

Leakage control

Every feature function is wrapped in a decorator that filters its input to gw < as_of_gw and then audits its output — a function can join its way back to the future, so checking inputs alone is insufficient. All backtesting is walk-forward; random k-fold on football panel data leaks the future and inflates everything.

Errors found, and what they teach

  • Element ids are reassigned every season. Joining on them matched different people. This corrupted the newcomer test set (23 → 244 once fixed, reversing the result) and put Haaland in a centre-back cluster. Football data has no stable natural key; use the code.
  • Position is a per-season attribute — FPL reclassifies players, so a carried-over role must be discarded, not inherited.
  • Start rates were computed over appearances, not games available. A reserve keeper with one 90-minute outing scored 1.00. All 20 clubs projected multiple starting goalkeepers.
  • Injured players counted as starters at 0.00 xPts, which made the "most overpriced" list a list of injured players.
  • 2022/23 has no xG before GW16, arriving as exactly 0.000 and being fitted as genuine goalless expectations.
  • Goalkeeper labels differ by season (GK vs GKP); unnormalised, every keeper silently scored zero.

The scoring engine reconstructs 109,681 player-matches at 100.00%, which is what makes these findable: any input error shows up as a residual rather than as a plausible-looking number. The one mismatch it ever produced was a real rule change — goalkeeper goals were worth 6 points in 2020/21 and are worth 10 now.

Known limits

  • Midfielders and forwards carry a measured residual bias of about −0.3 xPts per match, most likely unmodelled set-piece threat. Premium attackers are therefore somewhat understated, and the model's top fair price (£11.8m) sits below what FPL charges for elite forwards — some of that gap is this bias, not market error.
  • Gameweeks are simulated independently and accumulated: right for the mean, mildly understating variance, since it ignores serial correlation in form and rotation.
  • DefCon has one season of native data. Its joint relationship with clean sheets measures ~0 correlation, but the covariate that should drive it — opponent possession share — is not in the FPL feed.
  • League-strength coefficients for foreign signings are assumptions, not fitted values.
  • The price curve explains R² of only 0.23–0.35. Price is a weak predictor of points, which is precisely why mispricing exists — but it also means fair price is a central tendency, not a target.
