Threshold-Aware Monte Carlo Point Projection for Constrained Fantasy Sports Squad Selection

K. Saha1

Independent Research, Ithaca, New York, 14853, United States

A Monte Carlo projection system is developed for Fantasy Premier League, in which the majority of scoring rules are threshold functions rather than linear rates. Because the mean of a threshold is not the threshold of a mean, component expectations are unsuitable as direct inputs; the system therefore simulates 20,000 realizations per fixture and applies the scoring rules to each draw. Component models comprise chained binomial logits for participation, a Dixon–Coles bivariate Poisson for team scoring, empirical-Bayes shrunk shares for attacking returns, and a hierarchical negative binomial for defensive action counts. The scoring engine is validated by exact reconstruction of 109,681 historical player-matches. Walk-forward evaluation over four seasons demonstrates that the system achieves lower mean absolute error and higher within-position rank correlation than an ownership-weighted benchmark in four of four seasons. However, when the projections are subjected to the squad constraints of the game and maximized for expected points, the resulting advantage is not statistically distinguishable from zero. This dissociation is shown to arise from the objective rather than from the projections. A competition scored by rank depends on the second moment of the margin over the field, and that variance is shown to be linear in the squad selection rather than quadratic, because the ownership indicator is binary. Optimizing the resulting objective wins four seasons of four by an average of 72 points, at a negative variance coefficient: risk is worth carrying in players the field also holds and worth avoiding in players held alone, inverting the common heuristic that differentials win rank.

I. Introduction

Fantasy Premier League requires a competitor to select fifteen players under a budget constraint, a positional quota, and a limit of three players per club, and to field eleven of them each week. Points accrue from a published rule set. The forecasting problem is therefore not merely to rank players but to produce quantities that survive a constrained combinatorial selection.

A property of the rule set governs the modeling approach. Most scoring events are thresholds rather than rates: a clean sheet is binary; defensive contribution pays at ten or twelve qualifying actions and nothing below; goals conceded deduct one point per two conceded as a step function. For a dispersed outcome, the expectation of a threshold function is not the threshold function of the expectation. A system that projects component means and then applies the rules is biased, and the sign of the bias depends on the player's variance.

Accordingly the system presented here simulates. For each fixture, 20,000 realizations are drawn and the published scoring rules are applied to every draw, yielding a distribution of points rather than a point estimate. Section II describes the data layer and the point-in-time discipline required to avoid look-ahead. Section III presents the component models. Section IV describes assembly and valuation. Sections V and VI give the validation methodology and results. Section VII derives the rank objective and reports the margin variance identity on which it rests. Section VIII reports negative results, which constitute a substantial fraction of the findings and are presented at equal length.

II. Data Layer and Point-in-Time Construction

A. Sources and Coverage

Ten seasons of per-gameweek player records (2016/17–2025/26) were assembled into a columnar store, comprising 253,890 player-fixture rows.1 Expected-goals quantities are available from 2022/23 onward; defensive action counts are published for 2016/17–2018/19 and 2025/26 only, leaving a six-season gap. Shot-level coordinates, situation tags, and post-shot expected goals were obtained for 2025/26.2

Key discovery. The complete scoring table is published by the game's own application programming interface. It is therefore generated programmatically and compared against the stored copy in continuous integration, rather than transcribed by hand. This eliminates a class of silent error and renders a future rule change a no-operation.

B. Point-in-Time Enforcement

The dominant failure mode in panel-data backtesting is leakage: a feature computed at gameweek t that observes data from t or later. Every feature function is therefore wrapped in a decorator that filters its input to gameweek < t and, critically, audits its returned frame. Input filtering alone is insufficient because a function may rejoin the full table internally and thereby reintroduce the future. All evaluation is walk-forward; random k-fold partitioning of football panel data leaks the future and inflates every metric.

C. Identifier Instability

Player identifiers are reassigned by the governing platform between seasons. Joining on them across a season boundary silently associates records belonging to different individuals. This defect was detected three times during development, in each case producing plausible but incorrect output, and is resolved by joining exclusively on a stable player code. Positional classification is likewise a per-season attribute and must not be inherited.

III. Component Models

A. Participation

Playing time is modeled as two chained binomial logits, for P(appearance) and P(≥60 min | appearance), rather than a single ordered logit. Selection and in-match substitution have distinct drivers, and forcing both through a common linear index degrades calibration in both. Walk-forward over 86,755 predictions gives a Brier score of 0.0881, against 0.1124 for an exponentially-weighted start-rate baseline and 0.1974 for the base rate.

B. Team Scoring

Fixture goal expectations follow the Dixon–Coles bivariate Poisson,3 in which independent Poisson marginals are corrected on the four lowest scorelines:

λ = exp(αh − βa + γ),    μ = exp(αa − βh)(1)
τ(0,0) = 1 − λμρ,    τ(1,1) = 1 − ρ(2)

The correction is physically motivated: a side that establishes a lead reduces risk, and two sides level late both do so, producing an excess of 0–0 and 1–1 relative to independence. Omitting it systematically underprices clean sheets. Exponential decay e−ξt is applied with ξ tuned out of sample; the minimum of 1X2 log-loss occurs at ξ = 0.003 day−1, corresponding to a half-life of approximately eight months. Fitting on expected goals outperforms fitting on realized goals (log-loss 0.9978 against 1.0093), as expected-goals estimates aggregate all chances rather than only converted ones.

C. Attacking Returns

Player attacking output is modeled multiplicatively as a share of team output, not as a per-90 rate:

E[xGi] = xGteam × si(3)

The distinction is material. A per-90 rate already embeds the quality of the side in which it was accrued; multiplying it by a fixture-adjusted team strength counts team quality twice. A share is approximately invariant to team strength and therefore admits the multiplication. Shares are shrunk by empirical Bayes with the weight estimated per position from a variance decomposition:

w = n90 / (n90 + σ2w/σ2b)(4)

The estimated ratio differs strongly by position — 6.5 for midfielders against 15.7 for defenders and forwards — indicating that midfielders separate from positional peers more than twice as rapidly. Penalty expected goals are removed using shot situation tags and reintroduced as a distinct term attached to current designated duty, since the inclusive quantity inflates a taker's apparent open-play threat by as much as thirty percent.

D. Defensive Contributions

Qualifying action counts are modeled as hierarchical negative binomial with a log-minutes offset and position-varying dispersion. The Poisson alternative is not merely less accurate but systematically biased downward (−0.028 against −0.002), being unable to generate the right tail on which threshold payoffs depend. Two results govern usage. First, evaluating P(N ≥ threshold) at a nominal ninety minutes rather than at projected minutes yields a Brier score of 0.1270, inferior to the base rate of 0.1183; the threshold is reached by playing. Second, calibration degrades severely above P = 0.5, where a predicted rate of 0.606 corresponded to a realized rate of 0.188. Isotonic recalibration and a tail cap at 0.40 are applied, both fitted walk-forward.

IV. Assembly and Valuation

A. Joint Simulation

Components are combined by simulation rather than by summation of marginals. One scoreline is drawn per realization and both squads are played out against it, so that a clean sheet and a low goals-conceded count are the same event, and bonus — awarded on a rank of a deterministic score across all twenty-two participants — emerges structurally rather than by assumption. Summing marginal expectations reproduces the mean while destroying the covariance, and the covariance is what captaincy and rank optimization require.

B. Valuation by Reduced Cost

Player value is expressed as a linear-programming reduced cost rather than as points per unit cost:

surplusi = xPtsi − λB pricei − μP − replacement(5)

The dual μP on the positional quota is necessary: the game compels a fixed positional composition, so a forward is never selected against the entire market but only against other forwards. Omitting it produced positive valuations for 76 percent of goalkeepers against 1 percent of forwards, a statement about the constraint rather than about the players. Fair price is obtained by inverting an empirically fitted concave price–output relation, not by dividing surplus by λB; λB is a marginal rate at a constrained optimum, approximately one third of the market gradient, and the linear inversion produced a fair price of 13.3 for a player listed at 4.5.

V. Validation Methodology

The scoring engine is validated by exact reconstruction: realized points are recomputed from action counts for 109,681 player-matches spanning ten seasons, achieving 100.00 percent exact agreement. The single discrepancy encountered was traced to a genuine rule change, goalkeeper goals having been revalued from six to ten points, demonstrating that the procedure detects rule drift from data alone.

Projection quality is assessed by walk-forward evaluation against four benchmarks in ascending order of difficulty: a price heuristic, the platform's published form statistic, points per appearance to date, and an ownership-weighted benchmark representing consensus selection. Squad-level performance is assessed by simulating complete seasons under the full constraint set with one free transfer per gameweek and a four-point charge for each additional transfer.

The squad-level result does not follow from the projection-level result. The system prevails in two seasons of four, with a mean margin of +2.8 points per season and a pooled weekly margin of +0.089 points (95 percent confidence interval −3.15 to +3.33; p = 0.96). No detectable season-points advantage over consensus selection is demonstrated.

This dissociation was long taken as the principal finding. The constraint set — fifteen players, three per club, a fixed budget, eleven fielded — drives all competitors toward overlapping selections, and the consensus benchmark is not weak but represents the aggregated judgment of several million competitors, already near the ceiling the constraints permit. A thirty percent reduction in projection error is compressed into a margin smaller than one week's variance.

Section VII shows the dissociation had a different cause: not a ceiling on what projection accuracy can buy, but an objective that was maximising the wrong quantity.

A. Where the Accuracy Advantage Is Concentrated

Aggregate accuracy statistics conceal the shape of the advantage. Scored against a five-gameweek form benchmark over 87,007 player-gameweeks, the system reduces mean absolute error in four seasons of four, by 5.2 to 11.2 percent, yet loses mean per-gameweek Spearman correlation in three seasons of four. The two results are compatible. Sixty-one percent of rows are players who did not appear; across the full list the ranking question is largely whether a player features at all, which a form average answers nearly as well as a minutes model.

The advantage is concentrated at the top of the ordering, where selection occurs.

Calibration is imperfect in a specific direction. Projections in the 4 to 5 point band return 5.45 points; the system under-projects its own top end and, across four seasons, produced six projections above five points. Conditional on a 60-minute appearance and after dividing out the appearance probability, residual bias is positional: forwards −1.62, midfielders −1.11, defenders −0.17.

B. Directional Validity of the Valuation

The valuation identifies players projected above what their price implies. Restricted to established starters and compared against other starters within the same 0.5m price band, the most-underpriced quintile outscores its band 63.4 percent of the time over the following six gameweeks, against 20.1 percent for the most overpriced, with the hit rate rising monotonically across quintiles and holding in all four seasons. The rate scales with the magnitude of the call, from 15.4 percent for negative residuals to 68.0 percent above 2.0 points, crossing 50 percent where the residual crosses zero.

An uncontrolled version of this test returned 94.7 percent and was measuring appearances rather than price: mean appearance probability rises from 0.20 in the bottom decile to 0.91 in the top, and the comparison group contained non-appearing players scoring zero.

VII. Rank Optimization and the Margin Variance Identity

Expected points is the wrong objective for a competition scored by rank. Write the margin over the field as a sum over every player in the game,

margin = Σi (owni − EOi) pi(6)

where owni is an indicator of ownership and EOi is effective ownership, the fraction of the field holding the player with captaincy counted twice. Separating the terms shows that the expectation of the margin is maximized by maximizing expected points, so the two objectives coincide in the first moment. Rank, however, is a nonlinear function of margin: a high finish requires a large positive margin, not a positive one. The second moment therefore matters, and it is where the objectives diverge.

Margin variance appears quadratic in the decision variable and thus outside a linear program. It is not. Because owni is binary, owni2 = owni, and

(owni − EOi)2 = owni(1 − 2 EOi) + EOi2(7)

The second term is constant. Margin variance is linear in the squad choice, with coefficient (1 − 2 EOi), and is carried exactly by the same linear program that maximizes expected points. The objective becomes

maximize   Σi owni [ xPtsi + γ (1 − 2 EOi) σi2 ](8)

The sign of the coefficient carries the result. A player held by more than half the field has a negative coefficient: owning him reduces margin variance, because his returns and failures move the competitor and the field together. A differential carries the full positive coefficient.

The parameter was swept on four seasons of walk-forward simulation under the full constraint set.

The optimum is sharp and negative. Positive γ, which purchases margin variance, degrades monotonically to −1790 points at γ = 0.40. An earlier linear surrogate that paid a premium for scarcity, x̂i(1 + k(1 − EOi)), failed at every k > 0 for the same reason and is reported in Section VIII.

Two controls were run because a four-of-four result invites them. Removing the ownership coefficient and penalizing variance uniformly yields −711 points and one winning season, establishing that the effect is the ownership structure rather than risk aversion. Substituting contemporaneous for deadline-lagged ownership inflates the margin by 68 points, and the lagged figure is reported throughout.

The practical statement inverts a widely held heuristic. Differentials are conventionally held to win rank. Under this constraint set they do not: a failure by an unowned player costs rank, while a failure by a universally owned player costs nothing, the field having failed alongside. Risk is worth taking where the field takes it jointly and worth avoiding where it is borne alone.

VIII. Negative Results

Five hypotheses were tested and rejected. They are reported because the rejections are more informative than several of the positive results.

A. Market Blending

Combining the team model with de-vigged bookmaker closing odds yields an improvement of +0.00101 in log-loss (t = +0.58, 95 percent CI −0.0024 to +0.0044) over 758 fixtures. The team model adds no measurable information beyond the market. Its retention is justified solely because bookmakers do not quote fixtures at the horizon the planner requires.

B. Post-Shot Expected Goals as a Skill Measure

Placement quality, defined as PsxG less pre-shot expected goals on target, does not persist within a season (r = −0.007), nor does finishing (r = −0.065), against a control — expected goals per shot — persisting at r = +0.742. The control establishes that the test has power. Shot selection is a stable attribute; what a player does to the ball thereafter is not measurable at these sample sizes. Goalkeeper shot-stopping is likewise unmeasurable, and here the control also fails, so the correct statement is that one season is insufficient rather than that no skill exists.

C. Role Inheritance for Transfers

The hypothesis that an incoming player inherits the output profile of the role he fills was tested on 244 debutants and rejected; a position-by-price-tier prior is superior on every metric. Two causes are identified: the listing price already encodes the club's assessment of the signing's intended role, and a minutes-weighted role profile describes the first-choice incumbent rather than the arrival.

D. Rank Tilting

Writing the margin over the field as ∑i(owni − EOi) pointsi, the second term is independent of selection. Hence in expectation, rank maximization and points maximization are identical, and any differential advantage must arise from the non-linearity of rank in margin. Tilting valuation toward lightly-owned players by xPts(1 + k(1 − EO)) was tested across four seasons and is monotonically harmful for k ≥ 0.15, costing 856 points at k = 1. The mechanism is visible in the margin distribution: tilting increases weekly standard deviation from 18.4 to 20.6 while reducing the mean from +0.09 to −6.9, so the upper tail does not improve. Ownership correlates with quality. The failure is specific to the surrogate rather than to rank optimization itself: replacing it with the exact margin variance objective of Section VII reverses the sign of the optimal parameter and wins four seasons of four.

E. Opponent Possession as a Defensive Contribution Covariate

Defensive contribution is the weakest component of the projection, and opponent possession share is the covariate its specification identified as missing. The relationship is large and unambiguous: across 47,585 player-matches, an outfield player whose opponent holds 60 percent or more of the ball reaches the qualifying threshold 34.4 percent of the time, against 17.4 percent at 40 percent or less. Possession is also forecastable, unlike goals, with team means correlating 0.77 to 0.91 between consecutive seasons and 0.85 between the opening ten matches and the remainder. A rating difference with a home term predicts a fixture at r = 0.71 in walk-forward evaluation, and forecast rather than realized possession still moves the threshold rate from 17.5 to 32.1 percent.

It contributes nothing to the model. Against a baseline containing only the player's own rolling counts, the existing opponent-strength feature is worth +0.92 percent in Brier score; expected opponent possession is worth −0.03 percent, and including both is inferior to opponent strength alone. The two features correlate at only 0.112, so this is not collinearity. The information is already present in the player's own history: an exponentially weighted count of his recent defensive actions is a direct measurement of how much defending he does, and a team-level possession forecast is a noisier statement of the same fact.

The negative result is worth stating precisely because the football reasoning was sound and the covariate is real. A hypothesis can be true about the sport and useless to the model.

IX. Transfer Economics

A separate result concerns the four-point charge levied for additional transfers. Applying the apparently rational criterion — incur the charge when projected weekly gain multiplied by expected holding period exceeds four points — cost 349 points across four seasons relative to declining all such transfers, of which only 156 points were the charges themselves. Recovery is monotonic in the required margin and complete only at abstention. This is the optimizer's curse: the transfer selected is that with the largest estimated gain, and the largest estimate in a noisy set is biased upward, so the charge is paid to acquire estimation error. Raising the threshold does not resolve it, because the filter operates on the same contaminated estimate.

X. Conclusions

A threshold-aware simulation system was constructed and validated. Its scoring engine reproduces 109,681 historical player-matches exactly, and its projections are more accurate than consensus selection in four seasons of four.

For most of this work that accuracy did not convert into points. Maximizing expected points won two seasons of four with a mean margin of +2.8 and p = 0.96, and successive structural interventions — rank tilting, transfer horizon valuation, hit discipline — produced improvements measured in tens of points against a seasonal variance measured in hundreds. The natural reading was that the constraint set imposes a ceiling and consensus selection sits near it.

That reading was wrong, and the error was in the objective rather than in the projection. A competition scored by rank is not scored by expected points, and the difference lives in the second moment of the margin, not the first. Once margin variance is written correctly — and it is linear in the squad choice, not quadratic, because the ownership indicator is binary — the resulting objective wins four seasons of four by an average of 72 points, with the optimum at negative γ. The direction is the substantive finding: risk is worth carrying in players the field also holds, because the field carries it jointly, and worth avoiding in players held alone.

Two conclusions follow for practice. Projection quality was never the binding constraint, and the effort spent improving it was measurably misdirected; the same system, unchanged, produces a positive result under a corrected objective. And the value that projection accuracy does carry is concentrated at the top of the ordering rather than spread across it. The system loses aggregate rank correlation to a five-gameweek form average while its top selection outscores that benchmark by 1.68 points per gameweek, which is the difference between describing a population and choosing from it.

The methodological caution stands unchanged and now applies to the positive result as well. Four seasons yield a sign test at p = 0.06. Two controls were required before the result was credited: removing the ownership coefficient collapses it, and contemporaneous ownership inflates it by 68 points. Any claim of a demonstrated edge from a single-season backtest should be regarded as a reading of noise; the present work reached the opposite conclusion on its own single-season result once three further seasons were added.
